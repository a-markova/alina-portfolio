## Introduction

Hello World! The MSBA program is unique in that it allows students to select a number of electives to support their technical classes. I was curious to see how students rank these electives against one another (and the core classes). This is harder than it sounds; preferences are a tricky thing to assign a numeric value to. Some people never give out a ‘10/10’, while others give full points to every class. Maybe they suspect the surveys aren’t as anonymous as they claim to be… who knows!

**MaxDiff** (also called *Best-Worst Scaling*) sidesteps this problem by forcing trade-offs. On each screen of the survey, respondents see a small subset of items (four classes in this case) and pick the *most-preferred* and the *least-preferred*. Basically: “of these four, which would you most want to take, and which least?” People are very good at this kind of relative judgment. It’s like asking “chocolate or vanilla?” over and over with the options shuffled (chocolate vs. vanilla vs. caramel vs. rocky-road-max-diabetes-cotton-candy-mint…). Enough of those comparisons and you can recover a full preference ranking.

This analysis uses MaxDiff data collected from our cohort. One consideration is that the class the survey was distributed in is included in the analysis. The ten items are the ten core + elective MSBA courses at UCSD Rady. The goal is to estimate how strongly students prefer each course, using three methods of increasing sophistication:

1.  **Counts analysis**: the simplest possible summary: for each class, percent of times chosen best minus percent chosen worst.
2.  **Multinomial Logit (MNL) via Maximum Likelihood**: models the choice process explicitly and returns point estimates with standard errors.
3.  **MNL via Bayesian estimation (Metropolis-Hastings MCMC)**: same model, but returns a full posterior distribution, making uncertainty about derived quantities (like preference shares) easy to quantify.

All three methods should broadly agree on the ranking. The interesting part is where they disagree, and what each one adds over the simpler approach before it.

## Counts Analysis

The simplest way to summarize MaxDiff data is to ignore the model entirely and just count. For each item, compute:

- **% best**: fraction of times it was picked *best* out of all the times it was shown
- **% worst**: fraction of times it was picked *worst* out of all the times it was shown
- **Score**: % best − % worst

A score near +1 means the class is almost always chosen as the top pick when it appears; near −1 means it’s almost always the least preferred. Near zero means it’s either genuinely middling, or it’s polarizing (loved by some, disliked by others).

## From MaxDiff Data to MNL Choices

To go beyond counts, we need a model. The **multinomial logit (MNL)** assigns each class $j$ a utility parameter $\beta_j$. When a respondent sees four classes $\{a, b, c, d\}$, the probability they pick $b$ as *best* is the familiar softmax:

$$
P(\text{best} = b) = \frac{\exp(\beta_b)}{\exp(\beta_a) + \exp(\beta_b) + \exp(\beta_c) + \exp(\beta_d)}
$$

After the best pick, the worst pick comes from the three remaining items, but now with *negated* utilities, since choosing the least preferred is like choosing the best on an inverted scale:

$$
P(\text{worst} = c \mid \text{best} = b) = \frac{\exp(-\beta_c)}{\exp(-\beta_a) + \exp(-\beta_c) + \exp(-\beta_d)}
$$

So each task screen gives us **two** MNL observations: one for the best pick from 4, and one for the worst pick from the remaining 3.

One important wrinkle: the softmax only cares about *differences* in $\beta$, not their absolute values. If you add a constant to every $\beta$, all the choice probabilities stay exactly the same. This means the model isn’t identified unless we fix a reference. I set $\beta_1 = 0$ (AI Math & Analytics as the anchor) and estimate the remaining nine.

## MNL via Maximum Likelihood

The log-likelihood sums over all tasks. For each task, it adds the log-probability of the observed best pick and the log-probability of the observed worst pick (from remaining items with flipped utilities):

$$
\ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{\text{tasks}} \left[
  \beta_{j^*_b} - \log \sum_{j \in \text{shown}} \exp(\beta_j)
  - \beta_{j^*_w} - \log \sum_{j \in \text{shown} \setminus \{j^*_b\}} \exp(-\beta_j)
\right]
$$

<details>
<summary>Show code</summary>

```python
def get_beta_full(beta9):
    """Expand 9-vector to 10-vector with beta[0] = 0 (item 1 is reference)."""
    return np.concatenate([[0.0], beta9])

def log_likelihood(beta9):
    beta = get_beta_full(beta9)
    ll = 0.0
    for _, row in task_df.iterrows():
        items = row['items']   # list of 4 item numbers (1-indexed)
        best  = row['best']
        worst = row['worst']
        
        # Item indices into beta (item j -> beta[j-1])
        idx   = [i - 1 for i in items]
        utils = beta[idx]
        
        # Log-prob of best pick (softmax denominator = all 4)
        ll += utils[items.index(best)] - np.log(np.sum(np.exp(utils)))
        
        # Log-prob of worst pick from remaining 3 (with negated utilities)
        remaining = [i for i in items if i != best]
        r_idx     = [i - 1 for i in remaining]
        r_utils   = -beta[r_idx]  # negate for "worst" direction
        ll += r_utils[remaining.index(worst)] - np.log(np.sum(np.exp(r_utils)))
    
    return ll

# Maximize (minimize the negative log-likelihood)
# L-BFGS-B handles this scale of problem cleanly and gives reliable convergence
result = minimize(
    fun=lambda b: -log_likelihood(b),
    x0=np.zeros(9),
    method='L-BFGS-B',
    options={'maxiter': 5000, 'ftol': 1e-12, 'gtol': 1e-8}
)

beta_hat = get_beta_full(result.x)
print(f"Optimization converged: {result.success}")
print(f"Log-likelihood at MLE:  {-result.fun:.2f}")
```

# L-BFGS-B doesn't return an exact Hessian, so we compute it numerically
from scipy.optimize import approx_fprime

def hessian_numerical(f, x, eps=1e-4):
    """Numerical Hessian via finite differences."""
    n = len(x)
    H = np.zeros((n, n))
    for i in range(n):
        def grad_i(x_):
            return approx_fprime(x_, f, eps)[i]
        H[i] = approx_fprime(x, grad_i, eps)
    return H

## MNL via Bayesian Estimation

Now we fit the same MNL model, but instead of a point estimate we want the full posterior distribution over $\boldsymbol{\beta}$. The posterior is:

$$
\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \propto L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \cdot \pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})
$$

I use a weakly informative prior: $\boldsymbol{\beta} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\; 10 \cdot I)$. With a standard deviation of about 3.2 on each $\beta$, this prior is diffuse enough not to pull the estimates away from the likelihood, but informative enough to rule out absurdly large utilities.

To sample the posterior I use a **random-walk Metropolis-Hastings** algorithm. The sampler proposes a small random step from the current $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, computes the log-posterior at the proposal, and accepts or rejects based on the Metropolis ratio. Over many iterations, the accepted draws trace out the posterior.

<details>
<summary>Show code</summary>

```python
def log_prior(beta9):
    return np.sum(-0.5 * beta9**2 / 10)  # N(0, 10) on each of 9 betas

def log_posterior(beta9):
    return log_likelihood(beta9) + log_prior(beta9)

def metropolis_hastings(beta0, n_iter, step, seed=123):
    rng_mh = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    K = len(beta0)
    draws = np.zeros((n_iter, K))
    beta = beta0.copy()
    lp = log_posterior(beta)
    accepted = 0
    
    for t in range(n_iter):
        # Propose a step
        proposal = beta + rng_mh.normal(0, step, size=K)
        lp_prop = log_posterior(proposal)
        
        # Accept or reject
        if np.log(rng_mh.uniform()) < lp_prop - lp:
            beta = proposal
            lp = lp_prop
            accepted += 1
        
        draws[t] = beta
    
    return draws, accepted / n_iter
```

**What the three methods agree on:** SQL and Customer Analytics are clearly the most preferred courses. They top the ranking in all three methods, and by a good margin. At the bottom, Capstone Project and Marketing/Finance/Operations sit last (or near-last) consistently. Strong preferences give the clearest signal, so the top and bottom of a ranking are usually the most reliable part.

**Where they disagree:** The middle four or five courses shuffle depending on the method. Counts scores for courses like Business Analytics and BI Systems are close to zero, and small differences in the denominator (how many times each was shown) can flip their relative order. MLE and Bayes are more stable here because they use all the choice information, including what it means when a class is shown and not chosen.

**What MNL adds over counts:** Counts treats “shown and not chosen” as uninformative. MNL doesn’t. When item A is shown alongside a very strong item B and doesn’t get picked, that’s less damning than not getting picked against a weak field. The likelihood captures this because it uses the full softmax denominator, which reflects the quality of the competition in each task.

**What Bayes adds over MLE:** The MLE gives a single point estimate and standard errors. The Bayesian approach gives a full posterior distribution. This matters when you want uncertainty quantification on *derived* quantities. Getting a credible interval on the share of preference for “SQL” is easy with MCMC: just push each draw through the softmax and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. From MLE, you’d need the delta method, which involves a Jacobian. Bayes also scales naturally to hierarchical extensions, where you estimate individual-level preferences instead of one pooled model.

## Discussion

If I had to summarize this analysis for a student choosing their MSBA electives or an academic advisor designing the program: **SQL and Customer Analytics are the clear favorites, and Capstone and the combined Mktg/Fin/Ops course are reliably the least preferred.** That holds no matter which method you use. The middle of the ranking (Business Analytics, BI Systems, AI Math & Analytics) is less settled.

As for the methods, each one adds a bit more complexity and a bit more payoff:

- **Counts** is great when you need a quick number for a dashboard or a stakeholder update. It needs no modeling assumptions and is easy to explain.
- **MLE MNL** makes sense when you want a proper statistical model, with standard errors and a way to talk about the choice process instead of just tallies.
- **Bayesian MNL** is worth it when you need uncertainty on derived quantities like shares, want to estimate individual-level preferences with a hierarchical model, or want to bring in prior information from an earlier study.

A few caveats: this is a self-selected sample of students who are still in the program. People who dropped out or transferred aren’t in this data. Students in different quarters have experienced different subsets of these courses, which affects their priors going in. The program also has a big range of more technical and more soft-skill-oriented students. And the framing of the survey (“which course would you prefer?”) likely mixes “which course did I personally enjoy” with “which course do I think is most valuable,” which aren’t the same thing.

One could also extend this to a hierarchical MNL model to estimate individual-level utilities. That is a task for someone who has finished their capstone project! Possibly myself in circa 10 weeks.
