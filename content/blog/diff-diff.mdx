## Introduction

Does raising the minimum wage kill jobs? For a long time, the standard answer in economics was yes. Basic supply and demand says a binding price floor on labor should reduce employment. Card and Krueger (1994) pushed back on that.

Their setup was clean: on April 1, 1992, New Jersey raised its minimum wage from \$4.25 to \$5.05 per hour. Pennsylvania, right next door, left its minimum wage unchanged. Card and Krueger surveyed fast-food restaurants in both states before and after the increase, then compared employment trends across the “treatment” state (NJ) and the “control” state (PA).

The idea is that New Jersey and Pennsylvania share a border, similar labor markets, and similar fast-food industries. Bigger shocks, like a recession or a regional slowdown, should hit both states about equally. That makes Pennsylvania a reasonable stand-in for what would have happened to NJ employment if the wage hadn’t gone up.

This is a **difference-in-differences** design. Instead of comparing NJ before vs. after (which conflates the wage increase with any other time trend), or NJ vs. PA at a single point in time (which conflates the wage effect with any pre-existing differences), DiD takes the difference of differences:

$$
\hat{\tau}_{DiD} = (\bar{Y}_{NJ,\text{after}} - \bar{Y}_{NJ,\text{before}}) - (\bar{Y}_{PA,\text{after}} - \bar{Y}_{PA,\text{before}})
$$

What lets us read this causally is the parallel trends assumption: that NJ and PA employment would have moved together without the policy. You can’t test it directly, but the shared border and the single-industry focus make it more believable than in a lot of natural experiments.

What did they find? Employment in New Jersey actually *increased* relative to Pennsylvania after the hike. It was a surprising result, and it changed a lot of how economists think about labor markets.

Below, I replicate Table 2 (summary statistics), Table 3 (the core DiD estimate), and Table 4 column i (OLS regression). I also plot the parallel trends logic, since the paper only shows it in tables.

## Data

The dataset is available from [David Card’s website](https://davidcard.berkeley.edu/data_sets.html). The raw file is a space-delimited `.dat` file with 46 columns documented in the accompanying codebook. The sample used throughout is the balanced panel: restaurants observed in both wave 1 (February/March 1992, before the wage increase) and wave 2 (November/December 1992, after). That gives us 75 Pennsylvania stores and 309 New Jersey stores.

The two states look similar across prices, hours, and ownership structure. The one notable difference is that NJ stores were more likely to be paying exactly \$4.25 before the hike, which makes sense since the new minimum only applied in NJ.

## Table 3 Replication: The DiD Estimate

Table 3 is the core of the paper: the before and after means for each state, the change within each state, and the difference between those changes, which is the DiD estimate.

*Means with standard errors in parentheses.*

|                          | Pennsylvania | New Jersey   |
|--------------------------|--------------|--------------|
| FTE employment, wave 1   | 23.38 (1.39) | 20.43 (0.52) |
| FTE employment, wave 2   | 21.10 (0.97) | 20.90 (0.53) |
| Change (wave 2 – wave 1) | -2.28 (1.25) | 0.47 (0.48)  |

**Difference-in-differences: 2.75 (1.34)**

PA employment dipped slightly between waves while NJ employment rose. The DiD estimate, the effect we can attribute to the minimum wage increase, comes out positive at about 2.75 FTE workers per store. Standard theory says employment should fall when the minimum wage goes up, and here it went the other way.

## Table 4 Replication: OLS Regression

Table 4 runs the same DiD as a regression, which lets Card & Krueger add controls for chain and ownership type. The model is:

$$
\Delta \text{FTE}_i = \alpha + \beta \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{NJ}_i] + \varepsilon_i
$$

where the outcome is the change in FTE for store $i$ and the NJ coefficient is the DiD estimate. Column i has no controls; column ii adds chain dummies and ownership.

*Dependent variable: change in FTE employment (wave 2 – wave 1). Standard errors in parentheses. Omitted chain: Burger King.*

|                            | Column i       | Column ii      |
|----------------------------|----------------|----------------|
| Intercept (PA mean change) | -2.283 (1.036) | -1.828 (1.173) |
| New Jersey (DiD estimate)  | 2.750 (1.154)  | 2.785 (1.156)  |
| KFC                        |                | 0.241 (1.277)  |
| Roy Rogers                 |                | -2.588 (1.298) |
| Wendy’s                    |                | -0.191 (1.431) |
| Company-owned              |                | 0.363 (1.082)  |
| N                          | 384            | 384            |
| R-squared                  | 0.0146         | 0.0290         |

The intercept is the average employment change in PA, and the NJ coefficient is the same DiD estimate from Table 3. Adding chain and ownership controls barely moves the estimate, so the result isn’t coming from differences in which chains are in each state.

## Visualizing Parallel Trends

Card and Krueger present everything in tables, but I find the DiD logic easier to see in a chart. Below I plot average FTE employment in NJ and PA across both waves, alongside a counterfactual for NJ: what employment would have looked like had NJ followed PA’s trend instead of getting the wage increase.

![](/blog/diff-diff/cell-3-output-1.png)

The dashed line is the counterfactual: NJ employment declining slightly, in line with PA. Instead, NJ rose. The gap between the solid and dashed pink lines is the DiD estimate, roughly 2.75 FTE workers per store attributable to the minimum wage increase.

Parallel trends is still an assumption. We can’t prove NJ would have followed PA’s path without the policy, but it’s a reasonable one here: the states are adjacent, the fast-food industry looked similar in both, and Card and Krueger surveyed the same restaurants before and after.

## Takeaways

My numbers line up with the paper. The three core numbers from Table 3 (PA change ≈ -2.28, NJ change ≈ 0.47, DiD ≈ 2.75) match the published results, and the OLS regression in Table 4 gives the same estimate with a standard error around 1.2.

Why the design holds up as a causal claim:

- **Natural experiment**: NJ raised its minimum wage; PA didn’t. Card and Krueger didn’t have to randomize anything.
- **DiD structure**: Differencing out time trends and pre-existing state differences isolates the policy effect, under the parallel trends assumption.
- **Within-industry comparison**: Focusing on fast food reduces noise from industry mix. Both states had similar chains, similar ownership structures, and similar customer bases.

The finding challenged the textbook model of competitive labor markets and started a lot of research on minimum wages, monopsony, and labor market imperfections. Using policy differences across a state border as a natural experiment also became a common approach in empirical economics.
