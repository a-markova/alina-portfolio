## Introduction

Ask me what I do and I'll probably start with my job. Ask me what I love and you'll hear about painting, getting outside, whatever book I'm in the middle of (currently *Lonesome Dove*), and my two tabbies, Handsome and Diva.

I spend my days in data, so naturally I went looking for numbers. It turns out the case for hobbies is a lot stronger than "it's nice to have a life." Here's what the research says, and why I think it matters for how we work.

## Hobbies are good for your body

In a 2009 study of 1,399 adults, people who spent more time on enjoyable leisure activities had [lower blood pressure, lower cortisol, smaller waists, and lower BMI](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19592515/), and they rated their own physical health as better. Those links held after accounting for demographics.

The effect shows up in the moment, too. Researchers at UC Merced and Penn State had more than 100 adults carry a heart-rate monitor for three days and check in on what they were doing and how they felt. [Virtually all of them had lower stress and lower heart rates during leisure](https://news.ucmerced.edu/news/2015/relax-benefits-leisure-go-beyond-moment), and their moods were better.

My favorite one, as a painter: a Drexel study had 39 adults spend 45 minutes making art, then measured their cortisol. [About 75% of them had lower stress hormone levels afterward](https://drexel.edu/news/archive/2016/June/Art_Hormone_Levels_Lower), and prior art experience made no difference. You don't have to be good at it for it to be good for you.

## Hobbies are good for your mind

The biggest study I found looked at 93,263 adults aged 65 and older across 16 countries. People with a hobby had [fewer depressive symptoms, better self-reported health, more happiness, and higher life satisfaction](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10504079/). The pattern held from Spain to Japan to the US, and country-level differences explained very little of it.

As a data person, I have to say it: most of these studies show association, not causation. Healthier, happier people might simply have more time for hobbies. The authors of that study say so themselves. But their analysis over time pointed the same direction, and every study I read lined up.

## Hobbies build community

Some of the most important benefits of a hobby aren't about the hobby at all. They're about the people.

A meta-analysis of 148 studies and 308,849 people found that those with stronger social relationships had a [50% greater likelihood of survival](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article/info:doi/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316) over the study periods. The authors put that on par with well-established risk factors like smoking and drinking.

Making friends as an adult is hard. A run club, a pottery class, a book club, or a pickup league makes it easier, because you show up regularly with people who already like the same thing you do. That's half the work of friendship done for you.

## Work to live

The flip side is what happens when work takes up everything. The WHO and ILO estimated that working 55 or more hours a week, compared with 35 to 40, comes with a [35% higher risk of stroke and a 17% higher risk of dying from heart disease](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo). In 2016 alone, long hours were linked to 745,000 deaths.

I love my job. I care about owning my work and about growing in it. But work is one part of a life, not the whole thing.

Hobbies are where I get to be a beginner, where nothing has a deadline, and where nobody is measuring the output. That's the point. There's even research showing that people with creative hobbies [recover better from work and get rated higher on job performance](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/creative-hobbies-linked-with-job-performance.html), but I think of that as a bonus, not the reason. The reason is that I want to live a life I enjoy, not just a career I'm proud of.

So paint badly. Join the club. Take the long walk. Read the giant Western. Your health, your friendships, and probably even your work will be better for it. And even if they weren't, you'd still be happier, and that counts.
