## Introduction

Hello World (again)! This one isn't about regression models, I promise.

Ask me what I do, and I'll probably start with my job. Ask me what I *love*, and you'll get a much longer answer: painting, getting outside, whatever book I'm in the middle of (currently *Lonesome Dove*... it's long, so ask me again in a month), and my two tabbies, Handsome and Diva. They're less of a hobby and more of a lifestyle.

I spend my days in data, so of course I went looking for numbers to back this up. And there are a lot of them!

## Your body will thank you

A 2009 study pooled 1,399 adults and asked how often they did things they enjoy. The people who did more of them had [lower blood pressure, lower cortisol, smaller waists, and lower BMI](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19592515/). They also rated their own physical health as better. This held even after controlling for demographics (yes, I checked).

It shows up in real time, too. Researchers had more than 100 adults wear heart-rate monitors for three days and check in on what they were doing and how they felt. [Virtually all of them had lower stress and lower heart rates during leisure](https://news.ucmerced.edu/news/2015/relax-benefits-leisure-go-beyond-moment). Their moods were better too.

My personal favorite (I'm biased): a Drexel study had 39 adults make art for 45 minutes and measured their cortisol before and after. [About 75% of them had lower stress hormone levels afterward](https://drexel.edu/news/archive/2016/June/Art_Hormone_Levels_Lower), and prior art experience made *no difference*. Basically: you don't have to be good at it for it to be good for you. I find that very freeing.

## Your brain, too

The biggest study I found followed 93,263 adults aged 65+ across 16 countries. People with a hobby had [fewer depressive symptoms, better self-reported health, more happiness, and higher life satisfaction](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10504079/). Spain, Japan, the US... the pattern held almost everywhere.

Now, I'd be a bad data person if I didn't say it: most of these studies show association, not causation. Maybe healthier, happier people just have more time for hobbies! The authors say as much themselves. But their analysis over time pointed in the same direction, and so did every other study I read. I'll take it.

## Find your people

This might be the most important one, and it barely has anything to do with the hobby itself.

A meta-analysis of 148 studies (308,849 people!) found that people with stronger social relationships had a [50% greater likelihood of survival](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article/info:doi/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316). The authors compared it to well-known risk factors like smoking and drinking. That's a big deal.

And making friends as an adult is hard. There's no homeroom. A run club, a pottery class, a book club, or a pickup league gives you a reason to keep showing up around the same people, plus something to talk about. Most of the awkward part is already handled.

## Work to live (not the other way around)

Here's the other side of it. The WHO and ILO found that working 55+ hours a week, compared with 35 to 40, comes with a [35% higher risk of stroke and a 17% higher risk of dying from heart disease](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo). In 2016 alone, long hours were linked to 745,000 deaths. Yikes.

Don't get me wrong, I love my job. I care about owning my work and growing in it. But work is one part of a life, not the whole thing.

Hobbies are where I get to be a beginner. No deadlines, no dashboards, nobody measuring the output (which, coming from a BI analyst, is saying something). There's even research showing people with creative hobbies [recover better from work and get rated higher on job performance](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/creative-hobbies-linked-with-job-performance.html). Nice bonus! But it's not why I do it. I want to live a life I actually enjoy, not just a career I'm proud of.

## Will you remember this week?

Quick exercise. Think about the past week. Now the past month. How many evenings after work, or mornings before it, actually stand out?

...Not many? Same, sometimes.

Your brain holds on to what's new and lets the repeats blur together. A routine doesn't need to take up space in your mind, so it doesn't. That's how someone can work the same routine for 40 years, then wake up one day to realize life has flown by and they can't remember much of it. Kind of terrifying, honestly.

Variety seems to matter for how we feel, too. A 2020 study tracked people in New York and Miami by GPS for three to four months and texted them to check in on their mood. [On days when they moved between more varied places, they felt more positive](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/590831). The effect was strongest in people whose brains showed tighter links between regions tied to novelty and reward.

The good news? It doesn't take a trip to Europe. It just takes something that isn't the same as yesterday.

## A basic guide to hobbies: CCP

Not sure where to start? I think about it in three buckets, which I call **CCP**:

- **Connection:** time with people. Dinner with a friend, a call with your mom, a book club, a pickup game.
- **Creativity:** making something. Painting, a new recipe, writing, music, building something with your hands.
- **Physical movement:** getting your body going. A walk, a run, a dance class, yoga, a hike.

(If these sound familiar, they should! They line up with the social relationships study, the art and cortisol study, and the leisure and blood pressure findings above.)

My rule of thumb: any 30-minute activity that involves at least one of the three is pretty much guaranteed to leave you feeling satisfied. Hit two or three at once, like a dance class with friends or a group hike, and even better.

Why 30 minutes? It's short enough to fit before or after work, and long enough to turn a random Tuesday into one you'll actually remember.

## Takeaways

So paint badly. Join the club. Take the long walk. Read the giant Western. Your health, your friendships, and probably even your work will be better for it.

And if they weren't? You'd still be happier. For me, that's reason enough.
